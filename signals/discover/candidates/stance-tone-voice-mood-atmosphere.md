# Stance, Tone, Voice, Mood, and Atmosphere

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft; descriptive, not evaluative

## Orientation

Stance is the attitude toward a proposition, an addressee, or a subject that an
expression explicitly encodes through attitude, engagement, and graduation
resources. Tone is the attitude attributed to a producer or to a work toward
its subject and audience on cited evidence. Narrative voice concerns who
speaks or narrates, at which level and with which relation to the story, as
against who perceives. Felt mood is a diffuse, low-intensity, weakly
object-directed affective state induced in or ascribed to a receiver.
Atmosphere is an affective quality experienced as spatially extended over a
scene, place, or setting.

This entry describes attributed attitude and felt quality. It does not own
semantic content, the interpretation act, perceptual appearance, signal-level
prosody, or systematic situational variation of language.

The following homonyms are excluded by name: grammatical mood and modality;
grammatical voice and diathesis; phonological lexical tone; musical and
pictorial tone; phonatory voice; organizational voice as a dissent channel; the
meteorological atmosphere and the pressure unit; and Genette's narrative
"mood", which is focalization.

Focalization itself — who perceives, and how much narrative information is
selected or restricted through that perceiver — is out of scope here and is not
owned by any current entry or candidate. It is recorded as unowned residue
rather than as a closed gap, and `narrative-voice` is deliberately restricted to
who speaks.

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Stance vs. tone | both are evaluative positions | encoded by identifiable evaluation resources vs. attributed to producer or work on cited evidence |
| Tone vs. felt mood | both are affective | attitude of or in the work toward something vs. diffuse state induced in or ascribed to a receiver |
| Felt mood vs. emotion | both are affective states | diffuse, longer, weak or absent intentional object vs. brief, intense, object-directed with an action tendency |
| Felt mood vs. grammatical mood | homonym only | low-intensity diffuse affect vs. an inflectional category encoding modality |
| Atmosphere vs. felt mood | both are ambient | quality attributed to the space or scene and spatially extended vs. a state of the subject |
| Atmosphere vs. appearance | both are receiver-relative | ambient affective quality of a setting vs. how an object seems from a perspective |
| Narrative voice vs. focalization | both are narrative categories | who speaks, at which level vs. who perceives and how much is disclosed |
| Narrative voice vs. grammatical voice | homonym only | narrating instance vs. active, passive, or middle diathesis |
| Narrative voice vs. authorial style | both project a producer | the narrating instance inside the work vs. a recognizable feature signature across works |
| Tone vs. meaning | both are read off the same text | attitude toward subject and audience vs. semantic content and reference |
| Tone vs. register | both vary with situation | attributed attitude vs. co-occurring features functional for a situation of use |
| Attribution basis vs. tone | both concern the same claim | where the claim is grounded vs. what the claim asserts |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- The expression, work, performance, or place is identified before any
  affective claim is made.
- Every tone, mood, or atmosphere claim carries cited evidence and a stated
  attribution basis.
- Duration, intensity, and object-directedness are recorded factors of
  `felt-mood`, not separate senses, and the criterion used is stated.
- Register, genre, style, dialect, and idiolect remain context factors; they
  belong to the register, style, and genre candidate and are not senses here.
- Semantic content, interpretation, and ambiguity retain their existing owner.
- Prosody, encoding, and signal quality retain their existing owner.
- Perceptual appearance and perceptual organization retain the perception
  candidate as their owner.
- Organizational dissent and accountability retain their existing owner.
- Focalization — who perceives, and how narrative information is selected or
  restricted through that perceiver — is out of scope and is currently unowned;
  it is recorded as residue and is not claimed by `narrative-voice`.
- Competing attributions and cultural variation remain visible rather than
  resolved.

This entry stops before named genres, named periods, named schools, individual
authors, aesthetic quality judgments, sentiment scoring systems, affect
measurement instruments, and clinical mood states.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify the expression, work, performance, or place, its producer, and its
   receiver or audience.
2. State whether the question concerns encoded stance, attributed tone,
   narrative voice, felt mood, atmosphere, or the attribution basis.
3. Exclude the homonyms by name before analysis, especially grammatical mood
   and grammatical voice.
4. Record the textual, prosodic, visual, or spatial evidence that the claim
   rests on.
5. For stance, name the attitude, engagement, and graduation resources used.
6. For tone, name the target of the attitude and the audience for which it is
   claimed.
7. For felt mood, record duration, intensity, and object-directedness, and name
   the criterion that separates mood from emotion.
8. For atmosphere, record spatial extent, setting, and onset conditions, and
   do not convert it into a property of one object.
9. For narrative voice, separate who speaks from who perceives.
10. Retain competing readings, cultural variation, and later revision.
11. Route meaning, interpretation, signal, appearance, scoring, and situational
    variation to their existing owners.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | separates tone, mood, voice, and atmosphere as word senses | joins them by attribution locus rather than by intuition |
| Literary glossary | supplies teaching definitions of tone, voice, and atmosphere | requires cited evidence and an explicit attribution basis |
| Narratology | separates who speaks from who perceives | keeps the narrative homonym of "mood" out of the affective sense |
| Systemic-functional linguistics | supplies analyzable evaluation resources | uses stance as evidence for tone without equating them |
| Affect science | supplies duration and object-directedness criteria | records which criterion was applied instead of assuming one |
| Aesthetics of atmosphere | proposes ambient, spatially extended qualities | keeps atmosphere descriptive and outside perceptual ownership |
| Typological grammar | fixes grammatical mood and voice | supplies the homonym exclusion list |

## Failure signs

- tone is treated as the meaning of the text;
- stance and tone are used interchangeably with no evidence cited;
- grammatical mood, grammatical voice, or lexical tone is analyzed as affect;
- Genette's narrative "mood" is read as an affective state;
- felt mood is asserted without duration, intensity, or object-directedness;
- mood and emotion are collapsed;
- atmosphere is reduced to one object's property or to a perceptual illusion;
- register or genre is imported as a sense of this family;
- an attributed tone is reported as the producer's actual state without a
  stated basis;
- one culture's tonal convention is presented as universal.
