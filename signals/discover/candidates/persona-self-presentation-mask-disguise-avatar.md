# Persona, Self-Presentation, Mask, Disguise, and Avatar

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft; descriptive, not identity policy

## Orientation

Self-presentation is the situated performance through which an agent gives, and
gives off, impressions to an audience. A persona is a constructed public
identity adopted by an agent for an audience and a purpose, with its own
attributes and its own disclosure status. A design persona is a fictional
archetypal user, grounded in research data, that stands in for a real segment
during design. A mask is a visible covering or figure that enables assumption
of another identity or state within a convention. A disguise conceals or
falsifies identity attributes with deceptive intent. An avatar is a digital
figure that represents and is controlled by a user in a mediated environment.

This entry owns the relation between an underlying agent, the projected figure,
and the audience, together with the declared basis on which conduct by the
figure is attributed to the agent. It does not own entity identity, identifier
semantics, organizational role, accountability for conduct, anonymity,
linkability or re-identification, rendering, or perceptual appearance.

The following homonyms are excluded by name: the religious avatara as the
descent of a deity into worldly form; the Jungian archetype when used outside
the cited psychological sense; grammatical person; a persona as a legal person;
and the theatrical mask considered purely as stage property.

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Persona vs. entity identity | both name a subject | a constructed presented identity, plural per agent vs. continuity criteria for one entity |
| Persona vs. alias | both stand in for an agent | a presented identity with its own attributes and audience vs. a reference token for the same entity |
| Persona vs. organizational role | both bundle expected behaviour | an identity projected to an audience vs. an institution-assigned bundle of work and authority |
| Persona vs. self-presentation | both concern impressions | a stable constructed identity vs. the situated performance in one encounter |
| Persona vs. disguise | both are assumed identities | may be openly conventional and non-deceptive vs. requires concealment or falsification with deceptive intent |
| Disguise vs. anonymity | both reduce identifiability | active projection of a false attribute vs. absence of identification within a set |
| Disguise vs. pseudonymity | both substitute an identity | intent to mislead about who acts vs. a consistent substitute identifier that may be disclosed |
| Mask vs. disguise | both cover | a conventional artifact enabling transformation, often recognized as a mask vs. concealment aimed at non-detection |
| Avatar vs. autonomous agent | both are digital figures | driven in real time by a human user vs. driven by an algorithm |
| Avatar vs. persona | both are projected figures | a perceptible figure in one environment vs. an identity construct that may span media |
| Avatar vs. rendered representation | both are displayed | the agent-figure-audience relation vs. the representation and its fidelity |
| Avatar vs. avatara | homonym with a shared etymology | a user-controlled digital figure vs. the descent of a deity into worldly form |
| Design persona vs. research participant | both describe users | an evidence-grounded fictional composite used as a proxy vs. an actual person studied |
| Design persona vs. persona | both are called persona | a design artifact standing for a segment vs. an identity an agent projects |
| Attribution basis vs. accountability | both concern conduct by the figure | the declared basis on which conduct is attributed to an agent, and whether it is disclosed vs. who answers for the conduct, owned by the organization and accountability entry |
| Attribution basis vs. linkability | both connect figure and agent | a declared and possibly disclosed basis for attribution vs. the technical possibility of relating records to one person, owned by the privacy candidate |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- The underlying agent, the figure, the audience, and the medium are named
  before any claim about projected identity.
- Purpose and disclosure status are recorded; adopting a figure is not by
  itself deceptive.
- `disguise` requires concealed or falsified attributes and evidence of
  deceptive intent; without both it is a persona or a mask.
- `design-persona` requires a stated evidence basis; an ungrounded persona is
  recorded as unevidenced rather than treated as research.
- Effects of the figure on the agent's own behaviour are a recorded factor
  supported by experimental evidence, not an assumption.
- Anonymity, pseudonymity, identifiability, and linkability retain the privacy
  candidate as their owner, together with whether an attribution can technically
  be established and the re-identification risk of establishing it.
- Entity identity, identifier, name, and alias retain their existing owner.
- Organizational role, answerability, and accountability for conduct retain
  their existing owner; this family records only the declared attribution basis
  and its disclosure.
- Rendering and representational fidelity retain the model and representation
  owner; perceptual appearance retains the perception candidate.

This entry stops before platform identity policies, legal identity and
impersonation law, named platforms, named virtual environments, avatar
rendering technique, identity verification methods, psychological diagnosis,
and moral judgment of any particular concealment.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify the underlying agent, the figure, the audience, the setting, and
   the medium or platform.
2. State whether the question concerns self-presentation, persona, design
   persona, mask, disguise, avatar, or the attribution basis.
3. Exclude the religious avatara, the Jungian archetype outside its cited
   psychological sense, grammatical person, and legal person by name.
4. Record purpose and disclosure status before evaluating conduct.
5. List controlled attributes and, separately, concealed or falsified
   attributes.
6. For disguise, record the concealment target and the evidence for deceptive
   intent; do not infer intent from concealment alone.
7. For avatar, record who or what drives the figure in real time, and separate
   the figure from its rendering.
8. For design persona, record the research data, the represented segment, and
   the sampling limits.
9. Record duration, reversibility, and how many figures the agent maintains.
10. Record the declared attribution basis, whether it is disclosed to the
    audience, and who declared it.
11. Route identity, identifiers, role, accountability, anonymity, linkability,
    re-identification, representation, and appearance to their existing owners.

| Depth psychology | proposes the persona as a psychic compromise | cites the sense without importing a clinical claim |
| Design practice | supplies personas as user proxies | requires an evidence basis and records sampling limits |
| Communication research | reviews competing avatar definitions | fixes the human-driven against algorithm-driven boundary |
| Privacy terminology | defines anonymity, pseudonymity, and linkability | routes those senses out instead of duplicating them |
| Religious reference | describes the avatara and its traditions | excludes that sense by name rather than blending it |

## Failure signs

- a persona is recorded as an alias of the same entity;
- a persona is treated as an organizational role;
- any constructed figure is classified as a disguise;
- deceptive intent is inferred from concealment alone;
- anonymity, pseudonymity, linkability, or re-identification is re-owned here;
- an avatar and an autonomous agent are treated as the same figure;
- the digital avatar is conflated with the religious avatara;
- a design persona is used without a stated evidence basis, or is cited as if
  it were a research participant;
- the figure is assumed inert, ignoring documented effects on the user;
- an attribution is recorded with no declared basis, or the basis is treated as
  disclosed when it was not;
- attribution of conduct is treated as a finding of accountability;
- technical establishability of a link is decided here instead of routed to the
  privacy candidate;
- the entry is used to justify unmasking a person or to set identity policy.
