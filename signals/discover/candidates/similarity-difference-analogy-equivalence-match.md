# Similarity, Difference, Analogy, Equivalence, and Match

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft

## Orientation

Similarity states that independently identified subjects are alike in declared
respects. Difference records where they diverge after a stated alignment.
Equivalence treats them as the same under a specified relation, invariant, or
purpose without erasing their identities. Analogy maps structure from a source
to a target to support a tentative comparison or inference. Matching selects
correspondences among candidates under explicit rules. Substitutability asks
whether one subject can replace another for a role, direction, and tolerated
loss.

These roles are related, but none establishes unrestricted identity.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `similarity` | In which declared respects, and by which measure, are these subjects alike? | graded likeness claim |
| `difference` | After alignment, in which respects do the subjects diverge? | contrast or mismatch claim |
| `equivalence` | Under which relation, invariant, partition, or purpose are the subjects treated as the same? | criterion-bounded sameness |
| `analogy` | Which source-to-target mapping supports a structural comparison or tentative inference? | directional correspondence |
| `match` | Which candidate correspondence satisfies the declared constraints and decision rule? | selected mapping |
| `substitutability` | Can a target replace a source for a declared role and direction? | directional replaceability |
| `interchangeability` | Can each subject replace the other over a declared domain with acceptable loss? | bidirectional replaceability |

## Root factorization

```text
comparison-correspondence-use
  := independently identified subjects
   x representations, versions, context, and purpose
   x compared features, relations, functions, or outputs
   x alignment, normalization, basis, and scale
   x equivalence criterion, invariant, and closure properties
   x similarity or distance measure and direction
   x analogy source, target, mapping, and projected inference
   x match candidates, cardinality, constraints, and unmatched cases
   x substitution direction, preserved properties, tolerated loss, and reversibility
   x remaining differences, evidence, uncertainty, counterexamples, and review
```

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Identity vs. equivalence | both can support sameness language | one subject under an identity criterion vs. distinct subjects treated alike under a relation or purpose |
| Equality vs. similarity | both compare subjects | exact satisfaction of a criterion vs. graded or feature-relative likeness |
| Similarity vs. analogy | both relate subjects | shared respects or measured proximity vs. directional mapping of relational structure |
| Equivalence vs. match | both may select correspondence | declared sameness relation vs. procedure or result that pairs candidates |
| Match vs. identity | either may associate two records | selected correspondence under evidence and thresholds vs. established entity continuity |
| Substitutability vs. interchangeability | both concern replacement | one-way replacement vs. replacement in both directions |
| Difference vs. arithmetic change | either can describe divergence | general contrast after alignment vs. a quantity-valued subtraction or ratio |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- Both subjects must have independent identity before comparison.
- Every claim states purpose, compared respects, representation, and alignment.
- Equivalence states its relation and whether reflexivity, symmetry, and
  transitivity are intended.
- Analogy retains source, target, mapping, projected inference, and failed
  correspondences.
- Matching retains candidate sets, cardinality, thresholds or objective, and
  unmatched cases.
- Substitution retains direction, preserved properties, acceptable loss, and
  invalid cases.
- Entity identity, mathematical identity, arithmetic difference, semantic
  equivalence, and model validity remain with their existing owners.

This entry stops before catalogs of similarity metrics, matching algorithms,
ontology-alignment systems, tolerance standards, and domain-specific analogy
collections.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify the subjects, versions, representations, and comparison purpose.
2. Select the exact claim type: similarity, difference, equivalence, analogy,
   match, substitutability, or interchangeability.
3. State the compared features, relations, functions, or outputs.
4. Record alignment, normalization, basis, scale, direction, and exclusions.
5. For equivalence, state the relation, invariant, domain, and closure
   properties.
6. For analogy, map source relations to target relations and retain unsupported
   projected conclusions.
7. For matching, retain all candidates, constraints, ties, unresolved cases,
   and unmatched subjects.
8. For substitution, state preserved properties, tolerated loss, direction,
   reversibility, and failure examples.
9. Record remaining differences, evidence, uncertainty, counterexamples, and
   review state.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | defines similar, same, equal, analogous, equivalent, and matching | separates the operational questions each word asks |
| Thesaurus | lists likeness and contrast terms | prevents lexical proximity from implying identity |
| Mathematics reference | defines equality, metrics, equivalence relations, and matchings | carries formal properties without exporting them to every comparison |
| Record-linkage or matching method | selects correspondences under a model | retains candidate set, thresholds, unmatched cases, and uncertainty |
| Analogy theory | explains structural transfer | keeps projected inferences tentative and directional |

## Failure signs

- similarity is asserted without compared respects or a measure;
- equivalent subjects are relabeled as one entity;
- a match threshold is hidden;
- analogy is treated as proof;
- one-way substitution is called interchangeability;
- remaining differences disappear after a successful match;
- a semantic, mathematical, quantitative, or model-validity question is
  silently re-owned.
