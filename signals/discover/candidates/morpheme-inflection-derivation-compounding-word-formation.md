# Morpheme, Inflection, Derivation, Compounding, and Word Formation

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft; descriptive, not prescriptive

## Orientation

A lexeme is a vocabulary unit realized by a set of word forms; a word form is
the realization of a lexeme in a paradigm cell. A morpheme is a minimal
meaningful unit identified by a stated segmentation and identity criterion, and
its context-dependent realizations are allomorphs. Inflection realizes a
grammatical category value on a word form of the same lexeme. Derivation forms
a new lexeme from a base, often with a category or meaning change. Compounding
combines two or more lexical bases into one lexeme, usually with a head. Other
word-formation processes, such as conversion, reduplication, blending,
clipping, and back-formation, form new lexemes without ordinary affixation.

This entry owns word-internal structure and formation processes in language. It
does not own what a word means in a context, which forms a situation calls for,
generic recurrence, generic decomposition, the general-to-particular relation
as such, or identifier semantics.

The following homonyms are excluded by name and routed: shape-equivalence
morphology and set-theoretic image-operator morphology, whose dilation,
erosion, opening, and closing operate on shape and belong to the canonical
geometric entry; urban morphology, which remains unowned domain-specific
residue; Goethean morphology of organic form; and biological morphology as the
study of organism form.

The sense labels carry their own homonyms, which are also excluded by name:
inflection as the point at which a curve changes concavity, which belongs to
the geometric owner; compounding as compound interest and as chemical compound
formation; derivation as the derivation of a proof or of a unit within a
quantity system; and productivity as an economic ratio of output to input.

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Lexeme vs. word form | both are called words | the vocabulary unit realized across a paradigm vs. one realization in a paradigm cell |
| Lexeme and word form vs. general and particular | both relate a general unit to a case | a language-specific realization relation with paradigm cells vs. the general instantiation relation, owned by the type and token candidate |
| Morpheme vs. morph | both are segmentation units | the abstract minimal meaningful unit vs. its concrete realization in a form |
| Morph vs. allomorph | both are realizations | a segment of the form vs. one context-conditioned realization of the same morpheme |
| Morpheme vs. character sequence | both segment a written form | a minimal meaningful unit under a stated criterion vs. an encoding-level substring |
| Inflection vs. derivation | both add structure to a base | realizes a category value of the same lexeme vs. forms a new lexeme |
| Derivation vs. compounding | both form new lexemes | a base combined with an affix vs. two or more lexical bases combined |
| Compounding vs. syntactic phrase | both join lexical material | one lexeme with a head and word-level behaviour vs. a syntactic combination |
| Affixation vs. non-affixal formation | both form lexemes | an affix is added vs. conversion, reduplication, blending, clipping, or back-formation applies |
| Productivity vs. frequency | both count occurrences | availability of the pattern for new formations vs. how often existing forms occur |
| Productivity vs. regularity | both concern patterns | availability under restrictions vs. an observed cross-instance repetition, owned by the pattern candidate |
| Lexicalized vs. transparent formation | both were formed by a process | listed with idiosyncratic meaning or form vs. interpretable from base and process |
| Morphological analysis vs. generic decomposition | both split a whole | a language-specific analysis with linguistic criteria vs. a declared decomposition mode and combination contract |
| Morphological form vs. selected form | both concern the same word | how the form is built vs. which form the situation of use calls for |
| Historical origin vs. current analysis | both explain a form | etymological source vs. the synchronic structure a speaker's grammar supports |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- The language, variety, and period are recorded before any claim.
- The lexeme and the word form are separated before inflection or derivation is
  claimed.
- Every segmentation states its criterion and its evidence, and unsegmentable
  or portmanteau cases are recorded as such rather than forced.
- The inflection and derivation criterion in use is named; conflicting criteria
  are recorded rather than resolved by fiat.
- Head, base, and affix are identified explicitly where the process requires
  them.
- Productivity, restrictions, blocking, and lexicalization are factors, not
  senses of their own.
- The analysis-basis check is a mandatory guard: a morpheme-based result is not
  reported as model-independent.
- Typological values are reported with their sample and coding source.
- Cross-instance recurrence claims and their artifact checks route to the
  pattern candidate; the general-to-particular relation as such routes to the
  type and token candidate, which supplies only the general relation while
  lexeme and word form remain the language-specific case owned here;
  situational selection of forms routes to the register candidate; generic
  decomposition routes to its canonical owner.

This entry stops before individual languages, named grammars, reference
descriptions of particular languages, syntax, phonological rules, orthography,
prescriptive usage, language teaching, etymological narratives, and
computational morphological analyzers.

## Selection procedure

1. Record the language, variety, period, data source, and glossing convention.
2. Separate the lexeme from the word form and name the paradigm cell.
3. State whether the question concerns morpheme identification, inflection,
   derivation, compounding, another word-formation process, productivity, or
   the analysis basis.
4. Exclude geometric, image-operator, urban, Goethean, and biological
   morphology by name, together with curve inflection, compound interest and
   chemical compounds, proof and unit derivation, and economic productivity.
5. Segment only with a stated criterion, and record allomorphy, portmanteau,
   zero, subtractive, and templatic cases explicitly.
6. For inflection, name the category and value and the paradigm or syntactic
   requirement.
7. For derivation, name the base, the affix or process, and the category or
   meaning change.
8. For compounding, name the bases, the head, and the transparency of the
   result.
9. Record productivity with its restrictions, blocking, and known gaps.
10. Record the analysis basis and the evidence for the boundaries claimed.
11. Route recurrence, general-to-particular, situational selection, generic
    decomposition, and encoding questions to their existing owners.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | lists lexemes and some formations | separates lexeme, word form, and formation process explicitly |
| Morphology textbook | defines morpheme, inflection, derivation, and productivity | records the criterion used and keeps gradience visible |
| Word-and-paradigm account | analyzes forms without stored morphemes | makes the analysis basis an explicit factor |
| Compounding handbook | classifies compound types and headedness | keeps compoundhood language-relative |
| Typological survey | codes affix position, fusion, and synthesis over a sample | reports values with sample and coding limits |
| Glossing convention | standardizes morpheme-by-morpheme notation | treats notation as notation, not as analysis |
| Historical linguistics | traces the origin of a form | separates origin from synchronic structure |

## Failure signs

- a segmentation is asserted with no criterion or evidence;
- portmanteau, zero, subtractive, or templatic forms are forced into
  one-morph-per-meaning segmentation;
- inflection and derivation are separated without naming the criterion, or
  gradient results are reported as binary;
- lexeme and word form are used interchangeably;
- a compound is identified with no head analysis and no language-relative
  wordhood criterion;
- productivity is inferred from frequency;
- lexicalized forms are analyzed as if fully transparent;
- a morpheme-based result is reported as model-independent;
- a typological value is generalized beyond its sample;
- glossing labels are treated as analytical conclusions;
- etymology is presented as synchronic structure;
- geometric, image-operator, urban, Goethean, or biological morphology is
  analyzed here, or a sense label is used in its curve, financial, chemical,
  proof, unit, or economic homonym sense.
