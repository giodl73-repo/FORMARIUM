# Perception, Sensation, Appearance, Illusion, and Hallucination

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft; descriptive, not diagnostic

## Orientation

A stimulus is an environmental or bodily event that impinges on a receptor or
sensor. Sensory transduction converts aspects of that event into afferent or
neural signals. Perceptual organization groups and segments input; perceptual
content or presentation concerns what object, property, or scene is experienced
as present. Appearance records how things seem to a subject from a perspective
and context. An illusion involves a relevant external stimulus or object that
appears other than it is on a selected comparison basis. A hallucination is a
perception-like episode without the corresponding external stimulus or object.

The entry describes perceptual distinctions. It does not diagnose a person,
select one philosophy of perception, or own observation evidence.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `stimulus` | Which environmental or bodily event impinges on a receptor or sensor? | triggering event |
| `sensation-transduction` | How is the stimulus converted into an afferent or neural signal? | receptor-level conversion |
| `perceptual-organization` | How are inputs grouped, segmented, bound, or separated as figure and ground? | structuring process |
| `perceptual-content` | Which object, property, or scene is perceptually presented or represented? | world-directed percept |
| `appearance` | How do things seem to the subject from this perspective and context? | subject-relative seeming |
| `illusion` | Is a present stimulus or object experienced other than it is on the selected comparison basis? | misperception with stimulus or object |
| `hallucination` | Is there a perception-like episode without a corresponding external stimulus or object? | perception-like episode without stimulus or object |

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus vs. sensation | either participates in sensing | event or energy pattern impinging on a receptor vs. transduced bodily or neural response |
| Sensation vs. perception | either contributes to experience | receptor-level conversion vs. organized presentation of objects, properties, or scenes |
| Perceptual organization vs. content | either shapes what is experienced | grouping and segmentation process vs. what is presented or represented |
| Appearance vs. property | either may use the same description | how something seems to a subject vs. claim about the subject-independent object |
| Illusion vs. hallucination | either can differ from a selected external comparison | misappearance involving a relevant stimulus/object vs. perception-like episode without it |
| Hallucination vs. delusion | either may occur in clinical contexts | perception-like episode vs. belief-level content |
| Perceptual episode vs. observation record | a report may connect them | subject's episode vs. auditable record with method, time, and provenance |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- Perceiver, modality, context, and comparison basis are explicit.
- Stimulus, transduction, organization, content, appearance, illusion, and
  hallucination remain distinct.
- Generic observation, measurement, signal, provenance, and semantic
  interpretation retain their existing owners.
- Any recorded report routes to Claim and Evidence for observation and
  provenance custody.
- Competing common-kind, representational, sense-data, naive-realist, and
  disjunctivist theories remain visible.
- Hallucination is a descriptive symptom-level term, not a diagnosis.

This entry stops before named modalities, named illusions, named disorders,
diagnostic criteria, causes for an individual, and treatment.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify perceiver, modality, bodily or environmental source, context, and
   time.
2. State whether the question concerns stimulus, transduction, perceptual
   organization, perceptual content, appearance, illusion, or hallucination.
3. Record stimulus source and medium without inferring the later percept from
   the stimulus alone.
4. Record organization cues, perspective, prior context, and comparison basis.
5. For illusion, identify the relevant stimulus or object and exact mismatch.
6. For hallucination, state only the descriptive absence of a corresponding
   external stimulus or object; do not infer diagnosis or cause.
7. Preserve uncertainty, competing explanations, functional impact, and
   clinical context where supplied by an appropriate source.
8. Route recorded reports and provenance to Claim and Evidence.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | distinguishes sensation, perception, appearance, illusion, and hallucination | joins them through a bounded layer sequence |
| Philosophy of perception | develops competing accounts of perceptual content and kinds | preserves theory disagreement |
| Neuroscience or psychology reference | explains receptors, transduction, and organization | retains modality and study scope |
| Clinical reference | defines symptom and diagnostic context | prevents symptom language from becoming diagnosis |
| Observation standard | records observed property, result, procedure, and provenance | owns the record without re-owning the perceptual episode |

## Failure signs

- sensation and perception are treated as synonyms;
- appearance is recorded as an object property without perspective;
- illusion is classified without a comparison basis;
- hallucination is treated as proof of psychosis or another diagnosis;
- hallucination and delusion are collapsed;
- a recorded report becomes a second observation-evidence owner;
- one philosophy of perception is presented as settled.
