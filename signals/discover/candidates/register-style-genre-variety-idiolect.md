# Register, Style, Genre, Variety, and Idiolect

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft; descriptive, not prescriptive

## Orientation

A register is the set of lexical and grammatical features that co-occur because
of a situation of use, described through field, tenor, and mode, and
interpreted by the function those features serve. A genre is a conventional,
culturally recognized class of complete texts or works with expected structure,
owned by a discourse community. A style is the set of preferential or aesthetic
feature choices that distinguish a producer or work within a register. A
variety or dialect is user-defined, marking region, social group, or period. An
idiolect is the feature set attributable to one individual producer.

This entry owns describable variation in linguistic and formal features. It
does not own semantic content, interpretation, attributed attitude, encoding
and signal quality, entity classification, or evaluative scoring.

Diction, in the sense of word choice, is a feature-level factor inside
`register`, `style`, and `idiolect`, not a separate sense: register is already
defined by co-occurring lexical and grammatical features.

The following homonyms are excluded by name: vocal register as a phonatory
range; the processor register; the registry as a record of entries; style as a
typographic or citation style sheet; and genre as a marketing category.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `register` | Which lexical and grammatical features co-occur because of this situation of use, and which function do they serve? | use-defined variation |
| `genre` | Which conventional class of complete texts or works, with which expected structure, does this occurrence belong to? | conventional class membership |
| `style` | Which preferential or aesthetic feature choices distinguish this producer or work within a register? | preferential variation |
| `variety-dialect` | Which user-defined regional, social, or temporal variety is in play? | user-defined variation |
| `formality-level` | Which position on a declared formality or deference scale is selected, and by which convention? | declared scale position |
| `idiolect` | Which features are attributable to one individual producer rather than to a shared variety? | individual attribution |

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Register vs. genre | both are conventional text classes | co-occurring features functional for a situation of use vs. a culturally named complete-text class with expected structure |
| Register vs. style | both are feature-based | features functional for the situation vs. features preferred without being functionally required |
| Register vs. variety | both are language varieties | use-defined by what the producer is doing vs. user-defined by who the producer is |
| Register vs. formality level | both vary with situation | multidimensional co-occurring feature sets vs. one position on a declared scale |
| Register vs. tone | both are read off the same text | describable feature selection for a situation vs. attitude attributed to producer or work |
| Register vs. encoding | both concern form | situational feature selection vs. representation of data in a signal or format |
| Genre vs. structural move | both concern conventional form | membership in the class vs. one expected component within it |
| Genre vs. entity classification | both assign a class | community-owned conventional class of texts vs. an assigned classification of an identified entity |
| Style vs. idiolect | both attribute features to a producer | choices distinguishing a work or producer within a register vs. the feature set attributable to one individual |
| Idiolect vs. authorship attribution | both link features to a person | a described individual feature set vs. a forensic identification claim |
| Variety vs. correctness | both invoke norms | a described user-defined variety vs. a prescriptive judgment of correct usage |
| Variation dimension vs. pattern | both are recurring regularities | a corpus-derived co-occurrence dimension vs. a general recurrence relation with its own artifact check |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- The text, utterance, work, or corpus is identified, with producer, audience,
  medium, and situation of use, before a variation claim is made.
- Feature observations and a comparison baseline are required; a register or
  style label without features is not a claim.
- Field, tenor, and mode are recorded factors of `register`, not senses.
- Diction and other lexical choices are feature-level factors.
- Formality levels carry the convention that defines them; the ladder is
  heuristic and is not treated as a measured scale.
- Corpus dimensions are reported with the corpus and language that produced
  them; generalization routes to the sampling owner.
- Idiolectal attribution states its evidential limits and is never reported as
  identification of a person.
- Attributed attitude, felt mood, and narrative voice retain the stance and
  tone candidate as their owner.
- Word-internal structure and word-formation processes retain the morpheme and
  word-formation candidate as their owner; a morphological feature may be a
  recorded co-occurring feature of a register or style, but its segmentation,
  inflection or derivation status, headedness, and productivity are analyzed
  there and not here.
- Semantic content, interpretation, and translation retain their existing
  owner; encoding and signal quality retain theirs.

This entry stops before named genres, named varieties, named periods, named
schools, individual authors, prescriptive usage rules, language teaching
curricula, forensic authorship identification, and readability or quality
scores.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify the text, utterance, work, or corpus, the producer, the audience,
   and the medium.
2. State whether the question concerns register, genre, style, variety,
   formality level, idiolect, or the evidence for the claim.
3. Exclude the vocal, processor, registry, typographic, and marketing homonyms
   by name.
4. Describe the situation of use through field, tenor, and mode before naming
   a register.
5. Record the observed lexical, grammatical, and formal features and the
   baseline they are compared against.
6. Interpret co-occurring features by the function they serve in the situation
   rather than by a single formality axis.
7. For genre, name the discourse community, the expected structure, and any
   violated or mixed expectations.
8. For style, separate preferential choices from features the situation
   requires.
9. For variety, state whether the variation is user-defined or use-defined and
   avoid evaluative labeling.
10. For idiolect, state the population baseline and the attribution limits.
11. Route generalization, attributed attitude, interpretation, encoding, and
    scoring to their existing owners.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | separates register, style, genre, and dialect as words | joins them by what defines the variation and by whose convention |
| Corpus linguistics | supplies co-occurring feature dimensions | keeps dimensions tied to their corpus and language |
| Systemic-functional linguistics | supplies field, tenor, and mode | uses them as factors without adopting one theory as canon |
| Genre analysis | supplies community ownership, purpose, and moves | admits genre only as a class-membership question |
| Sociolinguistics | supplies style shifting and audience design | keeps use-defined and user-defined variation apart |
| Formality ladder | supplies named levels | records the convention and refuses to treat it as measured |
| Literary stylistics | describes authorial style and diction | keeps lexical choice as a feature, not a sense |

## Failure signs

- a register is named without observed features or a baseline;
- register and genre are used interchangeably;
- style is claimed for features the situation requires;
- variation is reduced to a single formality axis;
- a corpus dimension is reported as a universal variety;
- a user-defined variety is treated as a use-defined register;
- diction is promoted to a sense and duplicates feature description;
- a variety label carries a judgment of correctness or capability;
- idiolectal evidence is presented as authorship identification;
- attributed tone or mood is recorded here instead of with its owner;
- genre membership is asserted with no discourse community named.
