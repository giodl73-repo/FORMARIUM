# Trust, Credibility, Reputation, Legitimacy, and Reliance

Status: noncanonical candidate entry draft

## Orientation

Trust is a vulnerability-bearing relation in which a trustor accepts risk based
on a positive expectation of a trustee within a domain. Trustworthiness is a
property or assessment of whether that trust is apt. Credibility is an
audience's current judgment that a source or message is believable. Reputation
is a historically accumulated and socially transmitted evaluation. Legitimacy
can name either accepted rightfulness or justified rightfulness. Reliance is
action or dependence based on expected performance and can occur without trust.

These roles can support one another, but none guarantees truth, justice,
competence, or future performance.

## Sense table

| Sense | Governing question | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `trust` | Who accepts vulnerability to whom, for what task or domain, and on what expectation? | attitude or relation |
| `trustworthiness` | Which ability, reliability, motive, benevolence, or integrity makes trust apt? | property of trustee, source, or system |
| `credibility` | Why should this audience believe this source or message now? | believability judgment |
| `reputation` | Which historically accumulated evaluation circulates in a community? | socially distributed assessment |
| `legitimacy-descriptive` | Which audience treats this actor, institution, or procedure as rightful or acceptable? | conferred acceptance |
| `legitimacy-normative` | By which standards or reasons is that rightfulness justified? | justificatory status |
| `reliance` | What action or dependence is undertaken on expected performance, with what consequence if it fails? | dependence relation or action premise |

## Decisive distinctions

| Pair | Shared feature | Decisive distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Trust vs. trustworthiness | either concerns a trustee and expectation | accepted vulnerability by a trustor vs. property that may make trust apt |
| Trust vs. reliance | either can support action under uncertainty | vulnerability-bearing social or normative relation vs. dependence that may be purely instrumental |
| Credibility vs. evidence | evidence can affect credibility | audience-relative believability of a source/message vs. claim-relative support or contradiction |
| Credibility vs. reputation | reputation can affect credibility | current judgment about a source or message vs. accumulated community-carried evaluation |
| Descriptive vs. normative legitimacy | either concerns rightfulness | acceptance by an audience vs. justification under standards or reasons |
| Legitimacy vs. authority | authority can claim legitimacy | perceived or justified rightfulness vs. granted decision right |
| Certification vs. trustworthiness | certification can support an assessment | scoped attestation vs. broader, revisable property judgment |

## Dependencies and stopping boundary

- Every trust claim states trustor, trustee, task or domain, vulnerability,
  stakes, expectation, and available alternatives.
- Trustworthiness does not depend on being trusted.
- Reliance does not automatically imply goodwill, betrayal, or legitimacy.
- Credibility does not establish truth.
- Reputation does not replace current evidence.
- Descriptive legitimacy can exist without normative justification.
- Evidence, authority, compliance, certification, and technical dependency
  retain their existing owners.

This entry stops before catalogs of trust metrics, political doctrines,
reputation algorithms, certification programs, and named trustworthy-system
frameworks.

## Selection procedure

1. Identify the subject and whether the question concerns trust,
   trustworthiness, credibility, reputation, legitimacy, or reliance.
2. Record trustor or audience, trustee or source, exact domain, task, claim, or
   procedure.
3. State vulnerability, stakes, downside, discretion, alternatives, monitoring,
   and fallback.
4. Separate competence, reliability, motive, benevolence, integrity, and
   alignment rather than compressing them into one score.
5. Bind credibility to an audience, message, time, and evidence.
6. Bind reputation to a community, history, transmission process, and update.
7. For legitimacy, select descriptive acceptance or normative justification and
   record the audience, procedure, standards, and contestation.
8. Record the action taken in reliance and consequences of failure.
9. Preserve misplaced trust, unrecognized trustworthiness, reliance without
   trust, credibility without truth, and legitimacy disputes.
10. Record repair, revision, decay, appeal, and review.

## Reference Delta

| Reference form | Typical contribution | Lexicon candidate delta |
|---|---|---|
| Dictionary or thesaurus | distinguishes trust, belief, confidence, reputation, and legitimacy | makes their participants, objects, and failure conditions explicit |
| Philosophy of trust | develops accounts of vulnerability, motive, goodwill, or reliance | preserves competing theories rather than selecting one |
| Organizational trust model | supplies measurable antecedents and outcomes | retains domain and organizational scope |
| Reputation system | aggregates historical reports or behavior | keeps the score separate from current credibility and truth |
| Trustworthy-system framework | identifies risk-managed characteristics | prevents those characteristics from becoming a universal guarantee |

## Failure signs

- trust and trustworthiness share one field;
- reliance is always labeled trust;
- credibility is inferred from popularity alone;
- reputation is treated as current evidence;
- descriptive legitimacy is presented as moral justification;
- formal authority is treated as proof of legitimacy;
- certification is treated as timeless trustworthiness;
- a trustworthy-system label is presented as a guarantee.
